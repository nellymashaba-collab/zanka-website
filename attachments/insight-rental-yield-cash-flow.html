Investing

Understanding Rental Yield and Cash Flow

Key metrics every investor should know.

Two properties can look identical on paper and perform completely differently as investments. The difference usually comes down to whether an investor is looking at yield, cash flow, or both, because they answer different questions.

Gross rental yield

Gross yield is the simplest measure: annual rental income divided by the property’s purchase price (or current value), expressed as a percentage. It’s useful for a fast, first-pass comparison between properties, but it ignores costs entirely. Two properties with the same gross yield can have very different actual returns once expenses are factored in.

Net rental yield

Net yield subtracts operating costs, such as levies, rates, insurance, maintenance reserves and management fees, from annual rental income before dividing by the property value. This is the number that more accurately reflects what an investor actually keeps, and it’s the figure worth comparing across potential purchases rather than gross yield alone.

Cash flow

Cash flow is different from yield: it’s the actual cash left over each month after rent is collected and every expense, including the bond instalment, is paid. A property can have a respectable net yield and still be cash-flow negative if it’s highly leveraged, meaning the owner tops it up from other income each month.

Why both matter

  • Yield tells you how efficiently the asset itself generates income relative to its value
  • Cash flow tells you whether the investment is sustainable on a month-to-month basis
  • A high-yield property with poor cash flow can still strain an owner’s finances during vacancies or rate hikes
  • A lower-yield property in a strong-growth area may be a better long-term hold despite thinner monthly margins

A simple worked view

Consider a property purchased for R1,200,000, renting at R10,500/month (R126,000/year). Gross yield is roughly 10.5%. After levies, rates, insurance and a management fee of, say, R30,000/year in total, net income is R96,000, a net yield of 8%. Whether that also produces positive cash flow depends entirely on the bond repayment, which is the piece purchase-price comparisons alone will never show you.
